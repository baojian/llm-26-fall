Course overview

Draft for review · September 7, 2026. Proposed lectures, milestones, and assessment details. Read the revision rationale and topic changes.

Study how language models work, then use them to investigate a question worth answering. The course combines the NLP foundations and notebooks from the Spring 2026 edition with selected Stanford CS336 material on training, data, compute, and evaluation.

Each student conducts one individual project. The central questions are why the task matters, what remains uncertain, which experiment can resolve that uncertainty, and what the evidence supports. AI assistants are part of the workflow for coding, engineering, literature exploration, and iteration. Students remain responsible for the claims they submit.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain tokenization, language-model objectives, attention, and a working Transformer.
  • Train and diagnose a small model; reason about data quality and resource budgets.
  • Adapt or retrieve with a pretrained model and evaluate the result under controlled conditions.
  • Formulate an important question, compare plausible approaches, investigate failures, and communicate defensible conclusions in writing.

Preparation and teaching approach

Expected background: Python and an introductory machine-learning course, including basic probability, matrix operations, gradients, and train/validation/test splits. PyTorch experience is helpful; the early practical work includes a focused introduction. A preparation notebook covering arrays, tensors, and a simple training loop is planned before the Transformer unit.

The common core serves undergraduates and master's students. Topics marked ★ are extension choices. Master's students select one substantial extension for their project; undergraduates may also choose one. Continuous-time diffusion, custom GPU kernels, and distributed-training implementations are outside the required core.

Each meeting has three 45-minute periods: typically concepts and a worked example, an experiment and interpretation, then guided practice. Quizzes replace part of practice time. Short animations may illustrate BPE, attention, caching, and unmasking; students also work with the corresponding numbers and code.

Assessment

The draft retains the overall weights: participation 5%, quizzes 15%, practical assignments 40%, and individual project 40%. Code volume and model size earn no credit by themselves.

Participation 5%Quizzes 15%Practical assignments 40%Individual project 40%

Participation and quizzes

Participation uses short exit tickets and in-class polls; full credit at 80% or more submitted. Quizzes assess the common core already taught, using brief explanations, calculations, and interpretation of unfamiliar examples. They are closed-book and closed-assistant. Each of the four retained quizzes contributes equally to the 15% quiz grade. Advanced extensions are not tested.

Three practical assignments

  • A1 · Text and probability (10%). Released Week 1, due Week 4. A small tokenizer exercise, a bigram baseline, and held-out evaluation. Explain one representation or smoothing choice.
  • A2 · Build and investigate a small LM (20%). Released Week 6, due Week 9. Complete selected components in a supplied Transformer framework, train a tiny model, and compare one data or training intervention with a baseline. Submit correctness checks, training curves, and an interpretation.
  • A3 · Adapt and evaluate (10%). Released Week 11, due Week 14. SFT on a supplied pretrained checkpoint with correct response-token masking; compare with the unadapted model on a held-out task. Inspect both improvements and regressions. DPO or GRPO is an extension.

Assignments establish common skills and can seed the individual project. Reuse the code and data preparation, but identify the additional question and experiments credited to the project. Staff reference code/checkpoints are planned so an early implementation failure does not block later work. The tiny model in A2 teaches training mechanics; the supplied pretrained model supports meaningful adaptation experiments.

Individual course project

One project per student, centered on an important, interesting, and answerable question. Ambitious AI-assisted work is welcome. A strong project develops evidence through successive experiments and explains what changed in the student's understanding.

Choosing a question

  1. Importance: who needs the answer, and which decision could it change?
  2. Uncertainty: what do prior results leave unclear, and what competing explanations are plausible?
  3. Evidence: which baseline, dataset, metric, and controlled comparison can distinguish those explanations?
  4. Feasibility: what can be learned with the available compute, data, and semester time?

Projects may investigate data, tokenization, training, evaluation, adaptation, retrieval, inference, diffusion, or agents. Building a system, reproducing a result, and analyzing a failure are all valid approaches. Novelty is not required for the undergraduate core. A useful negative result can receive full credit when the experiment and analysis are sound.

Example questions: Does filtering improve a fixed model under a fixed token budget? When should a course-notes RAG system abstain? How does a decoding policy change accuracy and cost? These are starting points to narrow after reading related work, not claims of novelty.

Written checkpoints

  1. Week 7 · Proposal (one page). Motivation, relevant prior work, an uncertain question, baseline, evaluation plan, and approximate compute budget. Later-topic projects may use a supplied baseline before those lectures occur.
  2. Week 10 · Baseline and initial results (up to two pages). A working baseline, inspected evaluation examples, initial measurements, and one issue to resolve. Staff provide written feedback.
  3. Week 13 · Progress update (up to two pages). One controlled comparison, a finding or failure, a competing explanation, and the next experiment. Incorporate feedback in the final report.
  4. Week 17 · Final submission. At most four pages of main report, references and appendix unrestricted, plus code and a concise experiment record. Use the ACL template. Checkpoint text can be revised into the report.

No slides, posters, oral defenses, or group deliverables are required. Peer discussion is welcome; each student submits and is assessed on their own work.

Experiment record and proposed rubric

Record the configurations, data versions/splits, metrics, and artifact locations needed to trace the main results. Include a brief account of material AI assistance and key decisions: what was proposed, how it was checked, what was adopted or revised, and why. A complete prompt transcript is not required.

Master's students include one substantial extension and a deeper comparison with prior work. The extension can be theoretical or empirical; it need not concern diffusion. The same rubric applies, with the extension evaluated within the relevant criteria.

Policies

AI assistants

AI assistants are allowed for coding, engineering, debugging, conceptual explanations, literature exploration, and experimental iteration in practical assignments and projects. Students must verify sources, inspect data and outputs, validate key computations, and take responsibility for submitted conclusions. Disclose material AI use and distinguish measured results from proposed or generated examples.

Quizzes and explicitly designated concept checks are completed without AI. Other guided practical work may use AI. The course's policy applies to our adapted exercises; do not assume the original CS336 assignment policy is identical.

Academic integrity and submission

Follow the course academic-integrity policy. Attribute reused code, data, and ideas. Fabricated results and unattributed copying are misconduct. Submit work through eLearning by 23:59 China Standard Time on the listed date.
