Course overview

Learn how modern LLMs work: tokenization, language modeling, embeddings, attention, and Transformers; training and evaluation; data quality, compute costs, and retrieval. Learn to design controlled experiments and investigate failures. Materials are mainly online, drawing on my previous lectures and Stanford CS336. Each student explores one meaningful question through an individual project. AI assistance is welcome; verify results, explain decisions, and support conclusions with evidence.

Prerequisites: Python and introductory ML, including probability and matrix operations. Each meeting spans three 45-minute periods with explanations, worked experiments, guided practice, and occasional quizzes. We will use GitHub.

Assessment

Quizzes 10%Assignments 45%Individual project 45%

Quizzes: Quizzes assess the common core already taught, using brief explanations, calculations, and interpretation of unfamiliar examples. They are closed-book, and AI tools are not allowed. All five quizzes count, each contributing 2% of the course grade. Advanced extensions are not tested.

Assignments: Assignments establish common skills and can seed the individual project. Reuse the code and data preparation, but identify the additional question and experiments credited to the project. Staff reference code/checkpoints are planned so an early implementation failure does not block later work. The tiny model in A2 teaches training mechanics; the supplied pretrained model supports meaningful adaptation experiments. The three practical assignments are:

  • A1 · Text and probability (15%). Released Week 2, due Week 4. A small tokenizer exercise, a bigram baseline, and held-out evaluation. Explain one representation or smoothing choice.
  • A2 · Build a small LM (15%). Released Week 6, due Week 9. Complete selected components in a supplied Transformer framework, train a tiny model, and compare one data or training intervention with a baseline. Submit correctness checks, training curves, and an interpretation.
  • A3 · Adapt and evaluate (15%). Released Week 11, due Week 14. SFT on a supplied pretrained checkpoint with correct response-token masking; compare with the unadapted model on a held-out task. Inspect both improvements and regressions. DPO or GRPO is an extension.

Individual project: One project per student, centered on an important, interesting, and answerable question. Ambitious AI-assisted work is welcome. A strong project develops evidence through successive experiments and explains what changed in the student's understanding.

Choosing a question

  1. Importance: who needs the answer, and which decision could it change?
  2. Uncertainty: what do prior results leave unclear, and what competing explanations are plausible?
  3. Evidence: which baseline, dataset, metric, and controlled comparison can distinguish those explanations?
  4. Feasibility: what can be learned with the available compute, data, and semester time?

Projects may investigate data, tokenization, training, evaluation, adaptation, retrieval, inference, diffusion, or agents. Building a system, reproducing a result, and analyzing a failure are all valid approaches. Novelty is not required for the undergraduate core. A useful negative result can receive full credit when the experiment and analysis are sound.

Example questions: Does filtering improve a fixed model under a fixed token budget? When should a course-notes RAG system abstain? How does a decoding policy change accuracy and cost? These are starting points to narrow after reading related work, not claims of novelty.

Written checkpoints

  1. Week 7 · Proposal (one page). Motivation, relevant prior work, an uncertain question, baseline, evaluation plan, and approximate compute budget. Later-topic projects may use a supplied baseline before those lectures occur.
  2. Week 13 · Progress update (up to two pages). A working baseline, inspected evaluation examples, initial measurements, and one controlled comparison. Discuss a finding or failure, a competing explanation, and the next experiment. Staff provide written feedback; incorporate it in the final report.
  3. Week 17 · Final submission. The main report is limited to four pages; references and appendices have no page limit. Include code and a concise experiment record. Use the ACL template. Checkpoint text can be revised into the report.

No slides, posters, oral defenses, or group deliverables are required. Peer discussion is welcome; each student submits and is assessed on their own work.

Experiment record and proposed rubric

Record the configurations, data versions/splits, metrics, and artifact locations needed to trace the main results. Include a brief account of material AI assistance and key decisions: what was proposed, how it was checked, what was adopted or revised, and why. A complete prompt transcript is not required.

Policies

AI assistants: AI assistants are allowed for coding, debugging, and literature exploration in practical assignments and projects. Students must verify sources, inspect data and outputs, validate key computations, and take responsibility for submitted conclusions. Disclose material AI use and distinguish measured results from proposed or generated examples. Quizzes and explicitly designated concept checks are completed without AI. Other guided practical work may use AI.

Academic integrity and submission: Follow the course academic-integrity policy. Attribute reused code, data, and ideas. Fabricated results and unattributed copying are misconduct. Submit work through eLearning by 23:59 on the listed date.
