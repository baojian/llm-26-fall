1. Prepare and overview

Prepare a one-page proposal for an individual investigation. Survey-only projects are not allowed. Include an implementation, experiment, or tested artifact, with evidence that answers your question.

  1. Question and motivation. Explain what you want to learn, why it matters, and what prior work leaves unclear.
  2. First experiment. Name accessible data or a generator, a simple baseline, one comparison, and an evaluation that can answer the question. Keep development and evaluation examples separate.
  3. Feasibility. State CPU cores and RAM, GPU count and model, memory per GPU, storage, runtime per run, and total runs. Separate minimum hardware from optional parallel runs; total GPU-hours must include baselines and retries. Give a smaller CPU, one-GPU, or cached-output fallback and plan an early timing/memory pilot.

2. Resource requirements

Prefer CPU studies and short inference runs. Our GPU pool is shared. Choose the smallest hardware setup that can answer your question; one- and two-GPU plans should keep total use modest.

CPU · recommended starting point

Tokenizer and data audits, retrieval, verifier tests, or a measured cache-storage experiment. State CPU cores, RAM, and elapsed time; the minimum study needs no GPU.

1 GPU · inference or short training

Reuse a small checkpoint, train a 5M–30M teaching model, or fit adapters on a 0.6B model. Cards give a memory target per device and a total time estimate to check in a pilot.

2 GPUs · a bounded alternative

Run independent seeds or conditions in parallel, or test a supported draft/target split. Explain each device’s role and keep a one-GPU fallback. Two GPUs are optional in the suggested studies.

{{HIGH_DEMAND_GPUS}}+ GPUs · high demand

A project that needs this many devices is a poor fit for our small shared pool. Reduce the model, data, or number of runs before selecting it. Larger plans can use resources you obtain yourself; state their source and availability in the proposal.

Be careful with GPU usage; our course pool is limited.

4. Open-source contributions

Build and evaluate a useful contribution to an existing project: a task, verifier repair, model adapter, or tool. Include tests or a controlled comparison and describe your individual work.

Contribution routes and current limits

Instructor direction, October 9: substantive individual work accepted and merged into established, actively used open-source repositories will be considered for additional course-project credit. The exact points, cap, and treatment within or beyond the existing project maximum have not yet been specified. No automatic bonus is assigned by this catalog; the published rubric remains the basis for grading until a common policy is announced.

A new benchmark task, a verified evaluator repair, a useful model adapter, or a reusable dataset/tool can be a strong project. Agree on a useful contribution with upstream maintainers early. Keep a local experiment and report that answer the project question even if review takes longer than the semester.

For credit consideration, include the upstream PR and merge-commit links, maintainer acceptance, your individual contribution, tests and before/after evidence, and how review changed the work. A self-merged personal fork, raw PR count, or repository star count is not evidence of substantive external acceptance. This guidance does not promise a maintainer decision or paper authorship.

6. Others

Use other resources you can access

You may use your own computers or GPUs, laboratory or research-group machines, cloud credits, hosted APIs, and other resources you can obtain. You are not limited to the course pool or its proposed GPU-hour budget. State the resource source, availability, estimated cost, and total use in your proposal and experiment record.

Request only the portion you need from the shared course pool. Keep a smaller fallback if outside access is uncertain. The same rubric applies to everyone; a larger compute budget does not itself earn a higher grade.

Complete practical or experimental work

Students may not submit a survey-only project. Related work provides background. Your own contribution must include an implementation, experiment, tested dataset or benchmark, or evaluated repair, together with a baseline or other meaningful comparison and analysis. CPU projects qualify when they produce this evidence.
