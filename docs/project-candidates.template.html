Prepare your one-page proposal

  1. Question and motivation. Explain what you want to learn and why it matters. Read 2–3 relevant primary references and identify what remains unclear. For your own topic, find topic-specific papers beyond the planning links.
  2. First experiment. Name accessible data or a generator, a simple baseline, one comparison, and an evaluation that can answer the question. Keep development and evaluation examples separate.
  3. Feasibility. State the model size, memory requirement, total runs and GPU-hours, including baselines and retries. Give a smaller CPU or cached-output fallback and plan an early timing/memory pilot.

Choose for our shared compute pool

Prefer CPU studies and short inference runs. Our GPU pool is shared. Choose a project whose complete comparison can finish with limited access to one GPU.

CPU · recommended starting point

Tokenizer and data audits, retrieval, verifier tests, or a focused evidence review. The minimum study needs no GPU.

One GPU · inference

Reuse a small released checkpoint and cached outputs. Most cards target a total of 2–4 GPU-hours, including all conditions.

One GPU · short training

Use a 5M–30M teaching model or adapters on a 0.6B checkpoint. Check the full run plan with a small pilot before choosing this route.

Count preparation on a GPU, training, generation, evaluation, ablations, and reruns. Record actual memory and time; reduce the scope if the pilot does not fit.

Avoid projects that depend on multiple GPUs, prolonged LLM pretraining, large full-model tuning, online RL, or full benchmark reruns. The papers may use much more compute than the bounded experiment suggested here. Read them for methods and evaluation ideas; reproduce only the stated small study. Paid APIs and hosted environments also need an explicit cost estimate.

Contribution routes and current limits

Instructor direction, October 9: substantive individual work accepted and merged into established, actively used open-source repositories will be considered for additional course-project credit. The exact points, cap, and treatment within or beyond the existing project maximum have not yet been specified. A new benchmark task, a verified evaluator repair, a useful model adapter, or a reusable dataset/tool can be a strong project. Agree on a useful contribution with upstream maintainers early. Keep a local experiment and report that answer the project question even if review takes longer than the semester.

For credit consideration, include the upstream PR and merge-commit links, maintainer acceptance, your individual contribution, tests and before/after evidence, and how review changed the work. A self-merged personal fork, raw PR count, or repository star count is not evidence of substantive external acceptance. This guidance does not promise a maintainer decision or paper authorship.
