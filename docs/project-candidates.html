1. Prepare and overview

Prepare a one-page proposal for an individual investigation. Survey-only projects are not allowed. Include an implementation, experiment, or tested artifact, with evidence that answers your question.

  1. Question and motivation. Explain what you want to learn, why it matters, and what prior work leaves unclear.
  2. First experiment. Name accessible data or a generator, a simple baseline, one comparison, and an evaluation that can answer the question. Keep development and evaluation examples separate.
  3. Feasibility. State CPU cores and RAM, GPU count and model, memory per GPU, storage, runtime per run, and total runs. Separate minimum hardware from optional parallel runs; total GPU-hours must include baselines and retries. Give a smaller CPU, one-GPU, or cached-output fallback and plan an early timing/memory pilot.

2. Resource requirements

Prefer CPU studies and short inference runs. Our GPU pool is shared. Choose the smallest hardware setup that can answer your question; one- and two-GPU plans should keep total use modest.

CPU · recommended starting point

Tokenizer and data audits, retrieval, verifier tests, or a measured cache-storage experiment. State CPU cores, RAM, and elapsed time; the minimum study needs no GPU.

1 GPU · inference or short training

Reuse a small checkpoint, train a 5M–30M teaching model, or fit adapters on a 0.6B model. Cards give a memory target per device and a total time estimate to check in a pilot.

2 GPUs · a bounded alternative

Run independent seeds or conditions in parallel, or test a supported draft/target split. Explain each device’s role and keep a one-GPU fallback. Two GPUs are optional in the suggested studies.

3+ GPUs · high demand

A project that needs this many devices is a poor fit for our small shared pool. Reduce the model, data, or number of runs before selecting it. Larger plans can use resources you obtain yourself; state their source and availability in the proposal.

Be careful with GPU usage; our course pool is limited.

01 Whose text costs more tokens?

Audit modern tokenizers on English, Chinese, code, and mixed text.

Case studyResearch problem

Question: Which tokenization gaps persist when document type and meaning are controlled?

Compute: Laptop CPU, roughly 8–16 GB RAM.

Tokenizer files only; do not download model weights. Stream a small corpus sample.

Read first
  1. Qwen3.5 small model release — Qwen Team; 2026. Model card

    Inspect a current tokenizer and its exact configuration.

  2. FineWeb2: One Pipeline to Scale Them All — Penedo et al.; 2025. Paper

    Plan multilingual sampling and document-quality controls.

  3. ByT5: Towards a token-free future with pre-trained byte-to-byte models — Xue et al.; 2021 arXiv release. Research paper

    Understand the byte-level alternative and noise-robustness question.

Experiment plan, evaluation and smaller fallback
Minimum scope and data
Freeze 1,000 short documents from two languages and code; compare three released tokenizers including Qwen3.5. Add a 100-pair, manually checked English/Chinese parallel subset.
Baseline and controls
Byte and character counts, plus the course BPE tokenizer. Keep normalization and documents fixed.
Evaluation
Tokens per UTF-8 byte, tokens per character, paired token counts, round-trip failures, and robustness to spaces or Unicode variants. Token counts alone do not measure model quality.
Minimum complete outcome
A reproducible tokenizer audit with paired plots and 20 explained outliers.
Preparation
L01 tokenization; Python text processing and sampling.
Planning budget
A laptop with roughly 8–16 GB RAM. Use bounded data and cache repeated work. The minimum study needs no GPU; time a small CPU pilot before fixing the scope.
Risks and smaller fallback
Use 200 documents and two tokenizers; no model inference or training is needed.

04 Where do adaptive byte patches spend their resolution?

Study the BLT boundary rule using a CPU byte predictor.

Paper replication

Question: At the same average patch length, which code and multilingual patterns receive finer segments under entropy-based patching?

Compute: Laptop CPU, roughly 8–16 GB RAM.

Use a smoothed byte n-gram predictor on CPU. Study patch boundaries without training a BLT model.

Read first
  1. Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens — Pagnoni et al.; 2024 preprint / ACL 2025. Paper and official code

    Read the entropy-based patching mechanism; scope the study to boundaries.

  2. ByT5: Towards a token-free future with pre-trained byte-to-byte models — Xue et al.; 2021 arXiv release. Research paper

    Compare byte-level processing with learned token boundaries.

Experiment plan, evaluation and smaller fallback
Minimum scope and data
Fit a smoothed byte n-gram predictor on a separate training slice, then apply fixed-length and entropy-threshold patching to 1M held-out bytes. Use disjoint development text to match average patch length.
Baseline and controls
Fixed-size byte blocks at the same average patch length. Hold predictor and text fixed; count the predictor and patcher runtime.
Evaluation
Patch-length distribution, boundary locations around code/Unicode, allocation near high-surprisal bytes, round-trip correctness, and CPU time. These measurements do not establish downstream model quality.
Minimum complete outcome
A working patcher, a paired boundary audit, and an explanation of where it allocates finer segments. Label the n-gram predictor as an adaptation of BLT.
Preparation
L01 bytes and L02 n-grams; learn conditional entropy and development-set threshold selection.
Planning budget
A laptop with roughly 8–16 GB RAM. Use bounded data and cache repeated work. The minimum study needs no GPU; time a small CPU pilot before fixing the scope.
Risks and smaller fallback
Use 200 KB of text and a byte bigram predictor. Keep the boundary-allocation question; do not train a BLT model.

46 Can small training runs predict the next scale?

Test the reliability of a scaling prediction on tiny models before using it to allocate a larger budget.

Case studyResearch problem

Question: How well does a curve fitted to smaller runs predict a held-out larger configuration, and how sensitive is that prediction to the fitting choices?

Proposed ceiling: 24 GB memory target per GPU; up to 8 GPU-hours total across all devices and runs.

Minimum: one GPU running the four tiny configurations sequentially. Optional: use two GPUs for independent configurations or seeds. Two devices for two hours consume four GPU-hours; all runs share the eight-GPU-hour planning total. Pilot the smallest model and reduce token counts if needed.

Read first
  1. CS336 Assignment 3: Scaling — Stanford CS336 course staff; Spring 2026. Official assignment and local-training code

    Use the local scaling exercise as a starting point.

  2. Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models — Kaplan et al.; 2020 arXiv release. Research paper

    Understand the form and limitations of a fitted scaling curve.

  3. Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Hoffmann et al.; 2022 arXiv release. Research paper

    Distinguish model size, training tokens, and compute allocation.

Experiment plan, evaluation and smaller fallback
Minimum scope and data
Train one decoder family at roughly 5M, 8M, 12M, and 20M parameters on one fixed licensed corpus. Save checkpoints at 0.5M, 1M, and 2M training tokens; use two seeds if the pilot fits. Reserve the largest model for evaluating predictions rather than choosing the fitted curve.
Baseline and controls
Compare a predeclared low-parameter power-law fit with a simple log-linear extrapolation and a constant-loss predictor. Keep tokenizer, validation set, data order, and optimizer policy fixed; report any size-specific tuning.
Evaluation
Held-out loss-prediction error, ranking of candidate allocations, sensitivity to omitted fitting points and seeds, actual training time, and approximate FLOPs. Report uncertainty and distinguish interpolation from extrapolation.
Minimum complete outcome
