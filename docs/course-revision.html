Course progress and the remaining six lectures

Current progress: November 11, 2026

The instructor confirms that Lectures 8–10 have covered InstructGPT, SFT, reward models, REINFORCE/PPO, GRPO and DPO. These are prerequisites for the remaining course. The GRPO/DPO examples collected in the Lecture 09 supplement now serve as review; Lecture 11 will not repeat that introduction. Design decisions

  • Assume Python and an introductory machine-learning course. Teach the PyTorch details needed for the common exercises, and provide a preparation notebook.
  • Plan 16 teaching meetings. The Week 5 meeting moves from October 7 to Saturday, October 10. Teach Transformers there and advance later topics by one meeting. Use Week 16 for research synthesis and project revision.
  • Retain NLP foundations and the useful spring notebooks. Add explicit data, training, resource-accounting, and evaluation work from CS336.
  • Build on the completed post-training sequence. Use brief prerequisite checks instead of another SFT, PPO, DPO or GRPO derivation. Lecture 11 introduces new parameter-efficiency and teacher–student mechanisms.
  • Carry one small, versioned task collection through adaptation, retrieval, inference and agents where suitable. Keep its evaluation questions held out of teacher generation and tuning. Each class changes one declared factor and produces an interpretable comparison; students need not change projects.
  • Use one individual project per student. No presentations, posters, oral defenses, assigned project pods, or shared group deliverables are required.
  • Allow substantial AI assistance. Grade the question, design, evidence, interpretation, and reproducibility. More experimental iteration is encouraged; code volume and application feature count are not learning objectives.
  • Keep diffusion as one late course session and an advanced project option. Remove the requirement to interpret every early topic as a denoising process.
  • Write teaching materials in English, following the repository guidelines.

Lecture 11: Efficient adaptation and distillation

November 18 · 135 minutes. How can we transfer useful behavior under a small training budget? Start from the familiar SFT objective and independent evaluation boundary. Distinguish which parameters we train from where the training targets come from.

By the end, students should be able to count LoRA parameters, explain what QLoRA stores in low precision, distinguish sequence-level and token-level distillation, and design a comparison that measures both gains and regressions.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min Frozen base weights and low-rank updates; rank and target-layer choices; QLoRA's frozen quantized base versus trainable adapters E01, 8 min: trace a tiny matrix update, count trainable parameters and identify memory that LoRA does not remove
2, 45 min Teacher-generated responses versus soft next-token targets; temperature and KL direction; student-generated prefixes as an on-policy distillation bridge E02, 8 min: compare hard-target cross-entropy with a teacher–student loss and identify whose prefixes were sampled
3, 45 min Compare an unchanged checkpoint, ordinary adaptation and teacher-data adaptation; hold the student, token budget and evaluation protocol fixed E03, 15 min: audit supplied run records, separate a teacher-data change from a rank change, and report one gain and one regression

Read LoRA, Section 4 and QLoRA, Section 3. Generalized Knowledge Distillation supplies the bridge from fixed teacher responses to teacher feedback on student-generated sequences; its full training algorithm is optional reading.

Prepare a CPU matrix example, same-vocabulary probability fixtures and a small set of saved teacher responses with provenance. Token-level KL needs aligned token distributions; sequence-level text targets do not require identical tokenizers. Include a teacher error and a held-out failure slice. Teacher agreement alone is not evidence of task improvement. The classroom comparison may use clearly labeled supplied results; any live run is an optional small LoRA experiment, not a requirement to train a large teacher or repeat RLHF. This is ungraded practice supporting A3 and the project, with no new A3 rule.

Lecture 12: Retrieval and RAG

November 25 · 135 minutes. When should a model retrieve evidence, and which stage caused a wrong answer? Use a small course-notes collection with document IDs carried from retrieval through citation scoring.

Students should distinguish a knowledge-access problem from a parameter-update problem, locate failures in the pipeline, and justify a retrieval change with held-out evidence. Reuse Lecture 11's task protocol while changing access to documents rather than the model weights.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min BM25 and dense retrieval; query/document representations; Recall@k E01, 8 min: rank a small collection and identify missed supporting documents
2, 45 min Chunk boundaries, reranking and context assembly under a fixed budget E02, 10 min: compare two chunking or retrieval policies on the same questions
3, 45 min No retrieval, retrieved context and supplied supporting context; citation support and abstention E03, 12 min: separate retrieval failure, unsupported generation and unanswerable cases in saved responses

Read DPR, Sections 2–4 and RAG, Sections 2–3. Prepare checked relevance labels and answerable/unanswerable questions, then preserve a held-out split. The CPU route uses BM25, supplied embeddings and saved responses; a live generator is optional. Supplying known supporting context is a diagnostic control, not a deployable retrieval result. Keep the generator, prompt and context-token budget fixed when comparing retrieval policies. Save an error table with question ID, retrieved IDs, support label and failure category. The existing project progress update is due that evening; the classroom comparison adds no separate graded submission.

Lecture 13: Inference and computation at test time

December 2 · 135 minutes. How should a fixed inference budget be spent? Connect Lecture 07's compute allocation to the evaluation and verifier audits in Lectures 8–10. Use the retrieval workload from Lecture 12 as one concrete case. Separate systems efficiency from spending more attempts.

Students should estimate KV-cache memory, choose a metric that matches a workload, and compare deployed answer-selection quality at a declared cost.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min Prefill, decoding and KV-cache size; latency versus throughput; attention I/O E01, 8 min: estimate cache bytes from layers, KV heads, dimensions, sequence length, batch and dtype
2, 45 min Batching, cache reuse and quantization; warm-up and repeated measurements E02, 10 min: audit a timing/memory comparison for correctness, hardware and input/output token counts
3, 45 min Best-of-N, majority vote and verification; actual selection versus oracle pass@k E03, 12 min: plot selected-answer success against attempts, total tokens and wall time, including selection cost

Read FlashAttention, Sections 2–3, PagedAttention/vLLM, Sections 3–4, and Snell et al., test-time compute. Prepare a small CPU cache example and auditable candidate traces. An optional GPU profile must report its own hardware; it cannot turn a CPU trace into a measured GPU speedup. Report time to first token, inter-token latency and throughput separately. An answer selected with access to the correct answer is an oracle diagnostic, not the measured performance of a deployed verifier. A3 keeps its published deadline that evening; this class adds no requirement to rerun or extend the assignment.

Lecture 14: Agents and tool use

December 9 · 120 teaching minutes + 15 minutes for Quiz 5. When is a tool-use loop worth its additional cost? Extend Lecture 12's retrieval tool and Lecture 13's budget accounting into a bounded observe–act–tool loop.

Students should specify the agent state and stopping rule, recover from a tool error, and explain whether a measured gain comes from tools or simply from extra computation. PPO/GRPO are prior knowledge for an optional discussion of training on trajectories; the core implements and evaluates a loop.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min State, tool schema, observations and stopping conditions E01, 8 min: trace one successful episode and repair one failed tool call
2, 45 min Tool permissions, untrusted tool text, recovery and action limits E02, 10 min: inspect a prompt-injection fixture and enforce the permitted actions and stopping rule
3, 30 min + quiz Direct generation, a fixed retrieval pipeline and a bounded agent on the same tasks; then Quiz 5 E03, 10 min: compare success, failures, tool calls, tokens, latency and budget exhaustion

Read ReAct, Sections 2–3. SWE-bench is an optional evaluation-design case, not a required full benchmark run. Prepare an offline tool environment and replayable traces; network access and paid APIs are unnecessary for the core lesson. Include a timeout, a malformed tool result and an episode that exhausts its action budget. Use saved model decisions to make the CPU replay reproducible; distinguish replay validation from live agent performance. Training large agents or coordinating many agents is optional reading. Quiz 5 covers previously taught core material, not the new agent lesson or optional extensions; its questions remain in the instructor repository.

Lecture 15: Masked diffusion language models

December 16 · 135 minutes. What changes when a generator can update multiple positions? Retrieve BERT's masked prediction objective and the causal decoder as distinct starting points, then apply Lecture 13's cost accounting.

Students should connect a masking process to a denoising objective, execute an iterative sampler, and state what a fair comparison with autoregression can and cannot establish. This is one focused alternative-generation lesson.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min A discrete masking process and time-dependent denoising loss E01, 8 min: trace a corruption/target pair and explain why BERT alone is not a complete diffusion generator
2, 45 min Iterative unmasking, conditioning and sampling schedules E02, 10 min: trace token states and compare two unmasking schedules under the same toy denoiser
3, 45 min Compare quality, controllability and cost against autoregression; identify confounded model comparisons E03, 12 min: audit step count, forward passes, token budget, hardware and measured runtime in a supplied comparison

Read MDLM, Sections 3–4 and LLaDA, Sections 2–3. Prepare a CPU toy sampler with explicit assumptions and deterministic fixtures. Its traces illustrate mechanics, not language quality. Updating positions in parallel does not establish an end-to-end speedup; account for repeated forward passes. Real-model results need matched workloads and disclosure of model, data and training differences. No diffusion training or late project-topic change is required; students may use the supplied comparison for classroom practice.

Lecture 16: Research synthesis and project revision

December 23 · 135 minutes. Which project claims survive a careful check? Bring one current result, its baseline and its experiment record; students work individually and use the same criteria regardless of degree program.

Synthesize the course through a method-choice question: does the observed failure call for better data, adaptation, retrieval, more inference compute, tools, or a narrower claim? Students should justify that choice, identify a missing control, and write a reproducible statement of their strongest result.

Period Teaching route Student evidence
1, 45 min Map failure → method → comparison → evidence → claim using the course's adaptation, retrieval, inference and agent examples E01, 10 min: choose a justified intervention or narrow an unsupported inference
2, 45 min Inspect leakage, uncertainty, resource accounting and negative results E02, 12 min: repair a missing control or narrow a claim to the tested population
3, 45 min Revise figures, methods and limitations; check reproducibility E03, 15 min: prepare a revision checklist linking a report claim to code, configuration and experiment record

Use the project guidance, Lecture 10 experiment-report template and the project's own primary papers. Prepare an authored example report and an evidence checklist, keeping student work private. Provide the authored example as a fallback if a student's result is not ready. Prioritize analysis and revision of existing evidence over launching large new experiments. The report allows six pages of main content; references and appendices are excluded from the limit. Final submission remains December 30, 23:59 (Asia/Shanghai). No presentation, poster or new oral assessment is introduced.

Individual projects and AI assistance

Students choose a meaningful uncertainty rather than merely a list of features to implement. Building a system is welcome when it enables the investigation. The proposal establishes importance, prior work, plausible alternatives, evaluation, and feasibility. Careful replication, useful failure analysis, or a sound negative result can earn full credit.

Written milestones are Week 7 proposal, Week 12 progress update (including a baseline and initial results), and Week 17 final report. These replace the former posters, oral defenses, pod comparison pages, and mandatory reviewer roles. Students can reuse and revise checkpoint text in the final report. Peer discussion and individual office-hour consultations remain available.

AI assistance is allowed for implementation, engineering, research exploration, and iteration. Students verify sources, check computations, inspect examples, and own their conclusions. Keep a concise record of important decisions and traceable experiments; do not require a full chat transcript or an artificial list of rejected AI suggestions. Quizzes and designated concept checks assess individual understanding without AI.

The tiny model trained in A2 supports learning about pretraining. A supplied pretrained checkpoint supports SFT and application experiments. Projects on later topics can start from a supplied baseline; students need not wait for the relevant lecture or depend on their own early training run succeeding.
